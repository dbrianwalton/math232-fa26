    Objectives

    • Compare the areas of two triangles and a circular sector on the unit circle to bound \(\dfrac{\sin(\theta)}{\theta}\text{,}\) and use the Squeeze Theorem to find its limit as \(\theta\to 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Explain why radian measure, where a full turn is \(\tau=2\pi\text{,}\) is the natural way to measure angles in calculus.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{\theta\to 0}\dfrac{\cos(\theta)-1}{\theta}\) by multiplying by a conjugate, using a Pythagorean identity, and factoring out a known limit.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Use the two limits to obtain \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\sin(x)\) and \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\cos(x)\text{,}\) and differentiate expressions that combine sine and cosine with the product rule and the chain rule.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Use the quotient rule to find the derivatives of the other four trigonometric functions, and apply them together with the chain rule.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Last time the sum identities reduced the derivatives of sine and cosine to two limits, \(A=\displaystyle\lim_{h\to 0}\dfrac{\cos(h)-1}{h}\) and \(B=\displaystyle\lim_{h\to 0}\dfrac{\sin(h)}{h}\) (see Worksheet Exercise 6). Both limits have the form \(\frac{0}{0}\text{,}\) and we cannot use L’Hôpital’s rule to find them, because that rule needs the derivative of sine, which is exactly what we are trying to find. Instead we return to the unit circle. Today’s work will also show us which units we should use to measure angles.
    🔗

    1. Comparing Three Regions.

    The figure shows the first quadrant of the unit circle and an acute angle \(\theta\text{.}\) Three regions are shaded: a small triangle with height \(\sin(\theta)\text{,}\) the sector of the circle cut off by the angle \(\theta\text{,}\) and a large triangle with height \(\tan(\theta)\text{.}\) Both triangles have a base of length \(1\text{,}\) the radius of the circle.
    🔗
    Let \(\tau\) be the measure of one full turn around the circle. If angles are measured in degrees then \(\tau=360\text{,}\) and if angles are measured in radians then \(\tau=2\pi\text{.}\) In either case, \(\dfrac{\theta}{\tau}\) is the fraction of the circle covered by the rotation through the angle \(\theta\text{.}\)
    🔗

    (a)

    Find the area of the small triangle and the area of the large triangle, using \(\frac{1}{2}\cdot\text{base}\cdot\text{height}\) and the heights marked in the figure.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    The unit circle has area \(\pi\text{.}\) Explain why the area of the sector is \(\pi\cdot\dfrac{\theta}{\tau}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Look at how the three regions sit inside one another in the figure. Use this to write an inequality that orders the three areas from smallest to largest, in terms of \(\sin(\theta)\text{,}\) \(\theta\text{,}\) \(\tau\text{,}\) and \(\tan(\theta)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 95 Squeeze Theorem.

    Suppose \(g(x)\le f(x)\le h(x)\) for all \(x\) near \(a\) (except possibly at \(x=a\)), and suppose \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a}g(x)=L=\lim_{x\to a}h(x)\text{.}\) Then \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=L\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Squeezing \(\sin(\theta)/\theta\).

    The inequality from the last exercise is
    \begin{gather*} \frac{1}{2}\sin(\theta) \lt \pi\cdot\frac{\theta}{\tau} \lt \frac{1}{2}\tan(\theta)\text{,} \end{gather*}
    for \(0\lt\theta\lt\frac{\tau}{4}\text{.}\) We want \(\dfrac{\sin(\theta)}{\theta}\) in the middle. Because \(\theta\gt 0\text{,}\) we may multiply the left inequality by \(\dfrac{2}{\theta}\) without changing its direction, which gives \(\dfrac{\sin(\theta)}{\theta}\lt\dfrac{2\pi}{\tau}\text{.}\) In the right inequality we replace \(\tan(\theta)\) by \(\dfrac{\sin(\theta)}{\cos(\theta)}\text{.}\) Because \(\cos(\theta)\gt 0\) as well, multiplying by \(\dfrac{2\cos(\theta)}{\theta}\) gives \(\dfrac{2\pi}{\tau}\cos(\theta)\lt\dfrac{\sin(\theta)}{\theta}\text{.}\) Together,
    \begin{equation*} \frac{2\pi}{\tau}\cos(\theta)\lt\frac{\sin(\theta)}{\theta}\lt\frac{2\pi}{\tau}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    As \(\theta\to 0^+\text{,}\) the point on the circle approaches \((1,0)\text{,}\) so \(\cos(\theta)\to 1\text{.}\) Both outer expressions then approach \(\dfrac{2\pi}{\tau}\text{,}\) and Theorem 95 tells us that
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{\theta\to 0^+}\frac{\sin(\theta)}{\theta}=\frac{2\pi}{\tau}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    The argument used \(\theta\gt 0\text{,}\) but sine is odd (see Theorem 88), so \(\dfrac{\sin(-\theta)}{-\theta}=\dfrac{\sin(\theta)}{\theta}\text{.}\) The values for negative \(\theta\) match the values for positive \(\theta\text{,}\) so the limit from the left is the same, and \(\displaystyle\lim_{\theta\to 0}\dfrac{\sin(\theta)}{\theta}=\dfrac{2\pi}{\tau}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    2. Choosing the Units for Angles.

    (a)

    Suppose we measure angles in degrees. What is \(\displaystyle\lim_{\theta\to 0}\dfrac{\sin(\theta)}{\theta}\text{?}\) Give the exact value and a decimal approximation.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Which value of \(\tau\) makes this limit equal to \(1\text{?}\) Why would that choice make the derivative of sine simpler to write down?
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Radians.

    An angle is measured in radians when a full turn is \(\tau=2\pi\text{.}\) Then \(\dfrac{2\pi}{\tau}=1\text{,}\) and the arc of the unit circle covered by a rotation through \(\theta\) has length \(\dfrac{\theta}{2\pi}\cdot 2\pi=\theta\text{.}\) So the radian measure of an angle is the length of the arc it cuts off on the unit circle. With this choice,
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{\theta\to 0}\frac{\sin(\theta)}{\theta}=1\text{.} \end{equation*}
    From now on, all angles are measured in radians.
    🔗
    🔗

    3. The Limit of \((\cos(\theta)-1)/\theta\).

    We now find the second limit, using \(\displaystyle\lim_{\theta\to 0}\dfrac{\sin(\theta)}{\theta}=1\text{.}\)
    🔗

    (a)

    Multiply the numerator and the denominator of \(\dfrac{\cos(\theta)-1}{\theta}\) by the conjugate \(\cos(\theta)+1\text{.}\) Use the Pythagorean identity \(\sin^2(\theta)+\cos^2(\theta)=1\) to show that
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\cos(\theta)-1}{\theta}=\frac{-\sin^2(\theta)}{\theta\,(\cos(\theta)+1)}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Write the right side as a product of \(\dfrac{\sin(\theta)}{\theta}\) and one other factor that contains \(\sin(\theta)\) and \(\cos(\theta)\) but no \(\theta\) in a denominator.
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Use the limit of a product to find \(\displaystyle\lim_{\theta\to 0}\dfrac{\cos(\theta)-1}{\theta}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    The Two Fundamental Limits.

    For angles measured in radians,
    \begin{align*} \lim_{\theta\to 0}\frac{\sin(\theta)}{\theta} \amp = 1 \amp \lim_{\theta\to 0}\frac{\cos(\theta)-1}{\theta} \amp = 0\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    4. Derivatives of Sine and Cosine.

    Using the sum identities and the definition of the derivative, last time we wrote
    \begin{align*} \frac{d}{dx}\sin(x) \amp = \sin(x)\cdot\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\cos(h)-1}{h}+\cos(x)\cdot\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\sin(h)}{h} \\ \frac{d}{dx}\cos(x) \amp = \cos(x)\cdot\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\cos(h)-1}{h}-\sin(x)\cdot\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\sin(h)}{h}\text{.} \end{align*}
    Now we know the value of both limits.
    🔗

    (a)

    Substitute the values of the two limits to find \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\sin(x)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Substitute the values of the two limits to find \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\cos(x)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 96 Derivatives of Sine and Cosine.

    When \(x\) is measured in radians,
    \begin{align*} \frac{d}{dx}\sin(x) \amp = \cos(x) \amp \frac{d}{dx}\cos(x) \amp = -\sin(x)\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example: The Derivative of Tangent.

    The other four trigonometric functions are quotients involving sine and cosine (see Definition 85), so we can find their derivatives with the quotient rule. For tangent, \(\tan(x)=\dfrac{\sin(x)}{\cos(x)}\text{,}\) and
    \begin{align*} \frac{d}{dx}\tan(x) \amp = \frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{\sin(x)}{\cos(x)}\right] = \frac{\cos(x)\cdot\sin'(x)-\sin(x)\cdot\cos'(x)}{\cos^2(x)} \\ \amp = \frac{\cos(x)\cdot\cos(x)-\sin(x)\cdot\bigl(-\sin(x)\bigr)}{\cos^2(x)} \\ \amp = \frac{\cos^2(x)+\sin^2(x)}{\cos^2(x)} = \frac{1}{\cos^2(x)} = \sec^2(x)\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    🔗
    🔗

    Derivatives of the Trigonometric Functions.

    With \(x\) in radians, at every \(x\) where the function is defined,
    \begin{align*} \frac{d}{dx}\sin(x) \amp = \cos(x) \amp \frac{d}{dx}\cos(x) \amp = -\sin(x) \\ \frac{d}{dx}\tan(x) \amp = \sec^2(x) \amp \frac{d}{dx}\cot(x) \amp = -\csc^2(x) \\ \frac{d}{dx}\sec(x) \amp = \sec(x)\tan(x) \amp \frac{d}{dx}\csc(x) \amp = -\csc(x)\cot(x)\text{.} \end{align*}
    The derivatives of cotangent and cosecant follow from the quotient rule in the same way as tangent and secant. Notice that the derivative of each “co” function is negative.
