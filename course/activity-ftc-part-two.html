    Worksheet The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Part Two
    

    Objectives

    • State the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (Part 2) and use the evaluation notation \(F(x)\Big|_a^b\) to compute \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\) from any antiderivative \(F\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Write the antiderivative rules for \(e^x\) and \(\frac{1}{x}\text{,}\) and their chain rule versions for \(e^u u'\) and \(\frac{u'}{u}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Identify \(u\) and \(u'\) in an integrand, factor out constant multiples, and apply the matching antiderivative rule.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Check that the integrand is continuous on the interval of integration before applying the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    We now have enough antiderivative formulas to compute definite integrals exactly. Today we connect the accumulation functions we have been studying to a much faster way of evaluating integrals, and then practice the new antiderivative rules for the exponential and the logarithm.
    🔗

    Facts We Know.

    • An accumulation function \(A(x)=\int_c^x f(t)\,dt\) can be used to calculate any definite integral: \(\displaystyle \int_a^b f(t)\,dt=A(b)-A(a)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus guarantees that if \(f(x)\) is continuous, then \(A(x)\) is an antiderivative of \(f(x)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Any two antiderivatives \(F(x)\) and \(G(x)\) of \(f(x)\) differ by a constant on any interval where \(f(x)\) is continuous, meaning \(G(x)=F(x)+C\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 75. The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (Part 2).

    If \(F(x)\) is any antiderivative of \(f(x)\) and \(f(x)\) is continuous between \(a\) and \(b\text{,}\) then
    \begin{equation*} \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b)-F(a)\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 76. Evaluation Notation.

    The notation \(F(x)\Big|_a^b\) means \(F(b)-F(a)\text{.}\) With it, the theorem reads \(\displaystyle \int_a^b f(x)\,dx=F(x)\Big|_a^b\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    2. Antiderivative Rules for the Exponential and Logarithm.

    Every derivative rule corresponds to an antiderivative rule. The statement \(\frac{d}{dx}\big[F(x)\big]=f(x)\) is equivalent to \(\int f(x)\,dx=F(x)+C\text{.}\) We have two new basic derivative rules:
    \begin{align*} \frac{d}{dx}\big[e^x\big] \amp = e^x\\ \frac{d}{dx}\big[\ln(|x|)\big] \amp = \frac{1}{x} \end{align*}
    🔗

    (a)

    Write the integral statement that corresponds to each derivative rule.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    The chain rule versions of these derivative rules are
    \begin{align*} \frac{d}{dx}\big[e^u\big] \amp = e^u\cdot u'\\ \frac{d}{dx}\big[\ln(|u|)\big] \amp = \frac{u'}{u}\text{.} \end{align*}
    Write the integral statement that corresponds to each one.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 77. Sums and Constant Multiples.

    We still have the sum and constant multiple rules:
    \begin{equation*} \int c_1 f(x)+c_2 g(x)\,dx = c_1\int f(x)\,dx + c_2\int g(x)\,dx\text{.} \end{equation*}
    This means we can work on the individual terms of a sum, and we can factor out constant multiples.
    🔗
    🔗

    4. Identifying \(u\) and \(u'\).

    The chain rule factor \(u'\) must be identified and present in the integrand to use the antiderivative rule. We can factor out constant multiples, but we cannot factor out formulas involving the variable. For each problem below:
    • identify what is likely to be \(u\) and calculate the required \(u'\text{,}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • factor out any constant multiples that are not part of \(u'\text{,}\) and
      🔗
      🔗
    • use the antiderivative rule.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    (a)

    Find \(\displaystyle \int 4e^{2x}\,dx\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Find \(\displaystyle \int \frac{6x}{x^2+4}\,dx\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Find \(\displaystyle \int 2 e^{-3x} \,dx\text{.}\) (Hint: Multiply and divide to get \(u'\))
