    Worksheet Solving Exponential and Logarithm Equations
    

    Objectives

    • Solve equations with an unknown exponent by isolating the exponential expression and applying \(\ln\) to both sides, using \(e^u=N \Rightarrow u=\ln(N)\) and \(b^u=c \Rightarrow u\ln(b)=\ln(c)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Determine the constants in an exponential model \(y=Ae^{r(t-t_0)}\) from two data points, rewrite it in the form \(y=Ce^{rt}\text{,}\) and interpret doubling time and half-life.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Determine the exponent in a power law model \(y=A(x/x_0)^p\) from two data points, and distinguish a power law from an exponential model.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Rewrite any power \(u^w\) with \(u\gt 0\) as \(e^{w\ln(u)}\text{,}\) and use this form to express a power with a different base in terms of \(e\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Interpret \(\log_b(c)\) as the exponent \(u\) with \(b^u=c\text{,}\) evaluate simple logarithms such as \(\log_3(27)\) and \(\log_2(1/16)\) without a calculator, and use \(\log_b(c)=\dfrac{\ln(c)}{\ln(b)}\) to evaluate the rest.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Solve equations involving logarithms, checking the domain of each solution.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Now that we have both \(\ln(x)\) and \(e^x\text{,}\) and we know they are inverse functions, we can solve equations where the unknown sits in an exponent. This is the key skill for fitting exponential and power law models to data. The same idea also lets us write every power in terms of \(e\text{,}\) and every logarithm in terms of \(\ln\text{,}\) so that the natural exponential and natural logarithm are all we need for any base.
    🔗

    1. Solving for an Unknown Exponent.

    Recall from Definition 58 that \(e^u=N\) means exactly that \(\ln(N)=u\text{,}\) and that \(\ln(b^u)=u\ln(b)\text{.}\) Because \(\ln\) is one-to-one, applying it to both sides of an equation never creates or loses a solution, as long as both sides are positive.
    🔗

    (a)

    Solve \(e^{3x-1}=5\) for \(x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Solve \(4e^{-x/2}+1=13\) for \(x\text{.}\) What must you do before applying \(\ln\text{,}\) and why?
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Solve \(2^x=10\) for \(x\) by applying \(\ln\) to both sides. Which property of the logarithm moves the unknown out of the exponent?
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Solve \(5\cdot 3^{2t}=40\) for \(t\text{.}\) Write your exact answer using only \(\ln(2)\) and \(\ln(3)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (e)

    Explain why \(e^x=-2\) has no solution. What does this say about the range of \(e^x\text{?}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (f)

    A student solves \(3+2^x=11\) by “taking the logarithm of both sides” and writing \(\ln(3)+x\ln(2)=\ln(11)\text{.}\) Explain what went wrong. Then solve the equation correctly.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2. Fitting an Exponential Model to Two Data Points.

    An exponential model can be written \(y=Ae^{r(t-t_0)}\text{.}\) When \(t=t_0\text{,}\) the exponent is zero and \(y=A\text{,}\) so if one of our data points is \((t_0,A)\text{,}\) the constant \(A\) is already known. Only the rate \(r\) has to be found, and that is an equation with an unknown exponent.
    🔗
    A culture of bacteria is observed to have 400 cells at time \(t=2\) hours and 1500 cells at time \(t=5\) hours.
    🔗

    (a)

    Which data point should play the role of \((t_0,A)\text{?}\) Write the model with this choice, leaving only \(r\) unknown.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Substitute the other data point and solve for \(r\text{.}\) Is \(r\) positive or negative? Does that match the data?
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    How long does it take for the population to double? Set up the equation \(Ae^{r\,T}=2A\text{,}\) solve for \(T\) in terms of \(r\text{,}\) and then evaluate. Does your answer depend on the starting size \(A\text{?}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Rewrite your model in the form \(y=Ce^{rt}\) by using \(e^{a+b}=e^a e^b\text{.}\) What is \(C\) in terms of \(A\text{,}\) \(r\text{,}\) and \(t_0\text{,}\) and what does it mean in the context of the culture?
    🔗
    🔗

    (e)

    A drug concentration is measured as 12 mg/L at \(t=1\) hour and 3 mg/L at \(t=4\) hours. Build a model \(y=Ae^{r(t-t_0)}\text{,}\) find \(r\) exactly, and explain the sign of \(r\text{.}\) Then find the half-life, the time it takes for the concentration to drop to half of any given value.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3. Fitting a Power Law Model.

    A power law model can be written \(y=A\left(\dfrac{x}{x_0}\right)^p\text{.}\) Again, when \(x=x_0\) we get \(y=A\text{,}\) so one data point \((x_0,A)\) gives the constant, and the other gives an equation for the exponent \(p\text{.}\)
    🔗

    (a)

    The Earth is \(1\) astronomical unit (AU) from the Sun and has an orbital period of \(1\) year. Mars is \(1.524\) AU from the Sun and has an orbital period of \(1.881\) years. Take \((x_0,A)=(1,1)\) to model the period \(T\) as a power law in the distance \(a\text{:}\) \(T=\left(\dfrac{a}{1}\right)^p\text{.}\) Solve for \(p\text{.}\) What simple number is your answer close to?
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Suppose an animal of mass 4 kg uses energy at a rate of 14 W, and an animal of mass 64 kg uses energy at a rate of 112 W. Model the rate \(R\) as \(R=14\left(\dfrac{m}{4}\right)^p\) and find \(p\) exactly. Your answer should simplify to a rational number. Which property of logarithms makes that happen?
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    4. Every Power Is a Natural Exponential.

    By the definition of real powers, \(\ln(u^w)=w\ln(u)\) for \(u\gt 0\text{.}\) Reading this as an exponential equation gives
    \begin{equation*} u^w = e^{w\ln(u)} \qquad (u\gt 0)\text{.} \end{equation*}
    This lets us treat any power, including ones where both the base and the exponent vary, as a natural exponential.
    🔗

    (a)

    Rewrite each as a natural exponential \(e^{(\cdots)}\text{:}\) \(5^x\text{,}\) \(x^\pi\text{,}\) and \(x^x\text{.}\) For which values of \(x\) is each rewriting valid?
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    The drug concentration from before can be written \(y=12\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{2(t-1)/3}\text{.}\) Rewrite it using base \(e\text{.}\) How does your exponent compare with your model from Task 2.e?
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 60 Logarithm with Base \(b\).

    Let \(b\gt 0\) with \(b\ne 1\text{,}\) and let \(c\gt 0\text{.}\) The logarithm with base \(b\) of \(c\text{,}\) written \(\log_b(c)\text{,}\) is the exponent \(u\) that solves \(b^u=c\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \log_b(c)=u \quad \Leftrightarrow \quad b^u=c\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    5. A Logarithm Is an Exponent.

    (a)

    Without a calculator, find \(\log_3(27)\) and \(\log_2\left(\frac{1}{16}\right)\text{.}\) For each, write the equation \(b^u=c\) that you are solving.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Find each of \(\log_5(1)\text{,}\) \(\log_{10}(0.001)\text{,}\) \(\log_4(2)\text{,}\) and \(\log_{1/2}(8)\text{.}\) Which of these are negative, and why does the base or the input tell you so in advance?
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Use the definition to explain why \(\log_b(b^p)=p\) for every real \(p\) and \(b^{\log_b(x)}=x\) for every \(x\gt 0\text{.}\) What does this say about the functions \(b^x\) and \(\log_b(x)\text{?}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Between which two integers is \(\log_2(10)\text{?}\) It is not a nice exponent, so solve \(2^u=10\) using the method from Worksheet Exercise 1 and write \(u\) in terms of \(\ln\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (e)

    Repeat the previous task for a general equation \(b^u=c\) to find a formula for \(\log_b(c)\) in terms of natural logarithms.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 61 Change of Base.

    For \(b\gt 0\text{,}\) \(b\ne 1\text{,}\) and \(c\gt 0\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} \log_b(c) = \frac{\ln(c)}{\ln(b)}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    In particular, \(\ln(c)=\log_e(c)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    6. Solving Equations with Logarithms.

    (a)

    Use the change of base formula and Theorem 53 to explain why \(\log_b(xy)=\log_b(x)+\log_b(y)\text{.}\) Which other properties do you expect \(\log_b\) to share?
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Solve \(\ln(2x+1)=3\) and \(\log_2(x-1)=3\text{.}\) What do you do to both sides of each equation to undo the logarithm?
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Solve \(\log_2(x)+\log_2(x-2)=3\text{.}\) Combine the logarithms first, and then check each candidate solution against the domain of the original equation.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Summary.

    To solve for an unknown exponent, isolate the exponential expression, then apply \(\ln\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} e^u = N \amp \Rightarrow u = \ln(N) \\ b^u = c \amp \Rightarrow u\ln(b) = \ln(c) \ \Rightarrow\ u = \log_b(c) \end{align*}
    Every power with a positive base is a natural exponential, \(u^w=e^{w\ln(u)}\text{,}\) and every logarithm is an exponent, \(\log_b(c)=u \Leftrightarrow b^u=c\text{,}\) which can be computed from natural logarithms as \(\log_b(c)=\ln(c)/\ln(b)\text{.}\) When solving an equation with logarithms, undo the logarithm with the matching exponential, and always check that each solution lies in the domain of the original equation.
