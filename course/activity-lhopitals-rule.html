    Worksheet L’Hôpital’s Rule
    

    Objectives

    • Use the definition of the derivative and the sum rule for exponents to write \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\big[b^x\big]\) as \(b^x\) times a limit, and identify that limit as \(\ln(b)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Recognize limits of the indeterminate forms \(\frac{0}{0}\) and \(\frac{\infty}{\infty}\text{,}\) and explain why substitution alone cannot decide their values.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Verify the requirements of L’Hôpital’s rule before using it, and use it to replace a limit with a new limit formed from the ratio of derivatives.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Apply L’Hôpital’s rule repeatedly when the new limit is again an indeterminate form.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Rewrite products of the form \(0\cdot\infty\) as ratios, and rewrite limits with the variable in both base and exponent using base \(e\text{,}\) so that L’Hôpital’s rule can be used.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    We previously found the derivative of an exponential function by differentiating a logarithm. Today we will connect that result to the definition of the derivative, identifying a limit that none of our earlier techniques can evaluate. This will motivate a new tool for computing limits called L’Hôpital’s rule.
    🔗

    1. The Derivative of an Exponential, Revisited.

    Recall that the derivative of a function \(f\) is defined by
    \begin{gather*} f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} \text{.} \end{gather*}
    Let \(b\gt 0\) and let \(\exp_b(x)=b^x\text{.}\) We will apply this definition to \(\exp_b\text{.}\)
    🔗

    (a)

    Write the difference quotient for \(\exp_b\) and use the sum rule for exponents, \(b^{x+h}=b^x\cdot b^h\text{,}\) to factor \(b^x\) out of the numerator. Explain why \(b^x\) can then be moved outside the limit as \(h \to 0\text{.}\) Show that
    \begin{gather*} \exp_b'(x) = b^x \cdot \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{b^h-1}{h} \text{.} \end{gather*}
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Try to evaluate \(\displaystyle\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{b^h-1}{h}\) by substituting \(h=0\text{.}\) What do the numerator and denominator each do as \(h \to 0\text{?}\) Can you tell what the limit is from this information alone?
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    In earlier work, a limit of a quotient of simple algebraic formulas that gave an indeterminate form of 0/0 could be rewritten by canceling a common factor. Is there a common factor of \(b^h-1\) and \(h\) that you can cancel? Why is this limit different?
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Last time, we found a different formula for the same derivative: using \(y=b^x\text{,}\) we showed that \(\exp_b'(x)=b^x\ln(b)\text{.}\) Comparing the two formulas, identify the limit \(\displaystyle\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{b^h-1}{h}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 68. A Limit We Could Only Find Indirectly.

    For every \(b\gt 0\text{,}\)
    \begin{gather*} \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{b^h-1}{h} = \ln(b) \text{.} \end{gather*}
    We found this value recognizing the limit matches a derivative, not by evaluating the limit itself. A limit whose numerator and denominator both approach \(0\) is said to have the indeterminate form \(\frac{0}{0}\text{.}\) It is called indeterminate because the form alone does not decide the answer: such a limit might equal any number, or might not exist at all. We need to find a different formula for the limit, one that can be evaluated.
    🔗
    🔗

    2. Rewriting a Limit by Canceling a Common Factor.

    Consider the limit \(\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 2} \frac{x^2-4}{x^2-3x+2}\text{.}\)
    🔗

    (a)

    What happens to the numerator and the denominator as \(x \to 2\text{?}\) Why can we not simply evaluate the limit by substitution?
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Factor the numerator and the denominator. Identify the common factor and use it to rewrite the quotient as a simpler formula that agrees with the original for every \(x\ne 2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Explain why the limit of the original quotient as \(x \to 2\) is the same as the limit of your simplified formula. Why can the simplified limit be found by evaluation? What is the value of the limit?
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 69. L’Hôpital’s Rule.

    If \(f(x) \to 0\) and \(g(x) \to 0\) as \(x \to a\text{,}\) and \(\displaystyle\lim_{x \to a} \frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)} = L\text{,}\) then \(\displaystyle\lim_{x \to a} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = L\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 70. What the Rule Says and How to Use It.

    Canceling a common factor rewrites a limit of the form \(\frac{0}{0}\) as a new limit that we can evaluate. L’Hôpital’s rule does the same job for limits where there is no common factor to cancel: it lets us rewrite the limit using a new formula that we hope can be evaluated.
    🔗
    Notice that we are not computing the derivative of the quotient \(\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\text{,}\) and we do not use the quotient rule. We are creating a new limit whose formula is the ratio of the two derivatives \(f'(x)\) and \(g'(x)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The rule has two requirements. Requirement 1: \(f(x) \to 0\) and \(g(x) \to 0\) as \(x \to a\text{.}\) Requirement 2: the limit \(\displaystyle\lim_{x \to a} \frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}\) has a value \(L\text{.}\) We use the rule only after checking these requirements. Once they are satisfied, the rule is the justification for replacing the original limit with the simpler one.
    🔗
    🔗

    3. Checking the Requirements on a Familiar Limit.

    Return to \(\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 2} \frac{x^2-4}{x^2-3x+2}\text{.}\) Here \(a=2\text{,}\) \(f(x)=x^2-4\text{,}\) and \(g(x)=x^2-3x+2\text{.}\)
    🔗

    (a)

    Show that \(f(2)=0\) and \(g(2)=0\text{.}\) Since \(f\) and \(g\) are continuous, explain why this means \(f(x) \to 0\) and \(g(x) \to 0\) as \(x \to 2\text{,}\) so Requirement 1 is satisfied.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Find \(f'(x)\) and \(g'(x)\text{.}\) Form the new limit \(\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 2} \frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}\) and evaluate it. Does this limit have a value, so that Requirement 2 is satisfied?
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    State the conclusion that L’Hôpital’s rule gives for \(\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 2} \frac{x^2-4}{x^2-3x+2}\text{.}\) Does it agree with the value you found by canceling the common factor?
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 71. A Shorthand for Requirement 1.

    Checking Requirement 1 comes up so often that it helps to have a shorthand that does not require naming the functions. We write
    \begin{gather*} \lim_{x \to a} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} \rightarrow \frac{0}{0} \end{gather*}
    to mean that \(f(x) \to 0\) and \(g(x) \to 0\) as \(x \to a\text{.}\) The arrow reports what the numerator and denominator are doing. It does not say that the limit equals \(\frac{0}{0}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    4. A Limit with No Common Factor.

    Consider \(\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 2} \frac{3^x-9}{x^2-4}\text{.}\)
    🔗

    (a)

    Why can we not simply evaluate this limit? Can you cancel a common factor?
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Identify the numerator function \(f(x)\) and the denominator function \(g(x)\text{.}\) Does this limit satisfy Requirement 1? Record your conclusion using the shorthand \(\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 2} \frac{3^x-9}{x^2-4} \rightarrow \frac{0}{0}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Now that Requirement 1 is verified, find \(f'(x)\) and \(g'(x)\text{,}\) and write the new limit \(\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 2} \frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}\text{.}\) Can this limit be evaluated?
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    What is the value of \(\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 2} \frac{3^x-9}{x^2-4}\text{,}\) and which statement justifies your answer?
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    5. Using the Rule More Than Once.

    Some limits require repeating the process. Consider \(\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{e^{2x}-1-2x}{x^2}\text{.}\)
    🔗

    (a)

    Use the arrow notation to decide whether this limit satisfies Requirement 1.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    After this verification, create the new limit formed from the ratio of the first derivatives of the numerator and denominator. Try to evaluate it. What do you find?
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Verify Requirement 1 for this new limit using the arrow notation. After this second verification, create another new limit using the ratio of the second derivatives. Can this limit be evaluated?
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Now work backwards. Which of the three limits did you learn the value of first? Explain how L’Hôpital’s rule then gives the value of the middle limit, and then the value of the original limit.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 72. The Form \(\frac{\infty}{\infty}\).

    L’Hôpital’s rule also works when the numerator and denominator both grow without bound. If \(f(x) \to \pm\infty\) and \(g(x) \to \pm\infty\) as \(x \to a\) (or as \(x \to \pm\infty\)), and \(\displaystyle\lim \frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}=L\text{,}\) then \(\displaystyle\lim \frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=L\text{.}\) Requirement 1 is simply replaced by checking that the limit has the form \(\frac{\infty}{\infty}\) in the arrow notation.
    🔗
    🔗

    6. Limits at Infinity.

    (a)

    Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{100x}{e^{0.01x}}\text{.}\) Begin by verifying the form with the arrow notation.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 73. Products: The Form \(0\cdot\infty\).

    A limit of a product \(f(x)\,g(x)\) where \(f(x) \to 0\) and \(g(x) \to \pm\infty\) is also indeterminate. L’Hôpital’s rule only applies to ratios, so the first step is to rewrite the product as a ratio. Moving one factor to the denominator as its reciprocal produces a limit of the form \(\frac{0}{0}\) or \(\frac{\infty}{\infty}\text{,}\) and then we can check the requirements.
    🔗
    🔗

    7. Rewriting a Product as a Ratio.

    (a)

    As \(x \to 0^+\text{,}\) what does each factor of \(x\ln(x)\) do? Why is this limit not yet in a form where L’Hôpital’s rule applies?
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Rewrite \(x\ln(x)\) as a ratio by writing \(x=\dfrac{1}{1/x}\text{,}\) verify the form with the arrow notation, and find \(\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0^+} x\ln(x)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 74. Powers: Variable in the Base and the Exponent.

    A limit of the form \(\displaystyle\lim f(x)^{g(x)}\text{,}\) where the variable appears in both the base and the exponent, cannot be attacked directly. Instead, rewrite the expression using base \(e\) and logarithms:
    \begin{gather*} f(x)^{g(x)} = e^{g(x)\ln(f(x))} \text{.} \end{gather*}
    Then find the limit of the exponent \(g(x)\ln(f(x))\text{.}\) Because \(e^x\) is continuous, the limit of interest is \(e\) raised to the limit you found for the exponent.
    🔗
    🔗

    8. Variable in the Base and the Exponent.

    Consider the limit \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 0} (1+3x)^{2/x}\text{.}\) It is not in the form of a ratio where L’Hôpital’s rule would be relevant, so we will transform the formula before testing if it is indeterminate.
    🔗

    (a)

    As \(x \to 0\text{,}\) what limits do the base \(1+3x\) and the exponent \(\frac{2}{x}\) each appraoch? Use arrow notation to indicate this limit form.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Rewrite \((1+3x)^{2/x}\) using base \(e\) and \(\ln\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Find the limit of the exponent \(\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{2\ln(1+3x)}{x}\text{,}\) verifying the form first.
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Use the continuity of \(e^x\) to find \(\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} (1+3x)^{2/x}\text{.}\)
