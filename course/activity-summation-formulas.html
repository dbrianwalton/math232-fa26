    Worksheet Evaluating Riemann Sums with Summation Formulas
    

    Objectives

    • Discover, using a small checkable example, that a summation formula replaces the entire sum with a single expression, not each individual term.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Use the four basic summation formulas for \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^n a\text{,}\) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^n k\text{,}\) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^n k^2\text{,}\) and \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^n k^3\text{,}\) along with the sum rule and constant multiple rule to evaluate a summation without listing its individual terms.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Expand and simplify the summand of a right Riemann sum, then use the summation formulas to evaluate the sum as a single number for a specific \(n\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Repeat that process with \(n\) left as a symbol, treating \(n\) as a fixed (but unspecified) number rather than as the index of summation, to obtain a closed-form formula for the Riemann sum in terms of \(n\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Last class you learned to write a right Riemann sum using summation notation, such as \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n} f(x_k)\,\Delta x\text{,}\) without evaluating it. Today you’ll actually evaluate sums like this, first by hand-checking what a summation formula really claims, then by using a small toolkit of formulas to turn a Riemann sum into a single number, and finally into a single formula in \(n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Team Roles: Manager, Recorder, Spokesperson, and Reflector.
    🔗

    1. Does the Formula Really Replace the Sum?

    We start with a formula \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n} k = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}\) and you are asked to trust it for the rest of the activity. Before relying on it, check for yourselves what it actually claims.
    🔗

    (a)

    Compute \(1+2+3+4+5+6\) directly, by adding the six numbers one at a time.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Now substitute \(n=6\) into the formula \(\displaystyle\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\) and evaluate it. Compare this to your answer above.
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    A student claims that the formula \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n} k = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}\) means "replace every individual term \(k\) in the sum by \(\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\text{.}\)" Using your work above, explain why that is not what the formula says. What does \(\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\) actually replace?
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 24 Four Summation Formulas.

    The following four formulas are given as facts. (Each can each be proved, but proving them is not a course goal.) In each, \(a\) is a number that does not depend on \(k\text{,}\) and \(n\) is the number of terms being added.
    🔗
    • \(\displaystyle \displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n} a = n\cdot a\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle \displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n} k = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle \displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n} k^2 = \frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle \displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n} k^3 = \frac{n^2(n+1)^2}{4}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Definition 25 Sum Rule and Constant Multiple Rule.

    Summations also split over addition and let a constant factor be pulled out, the same way you already expect from other kinds of sums:
    🔗
    • \(\displaystyle \displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n} (a_k + b_k) = \Big(\sum_{k=1}^{n} a_k\Big) + \Big(\sum_{k=1}^{n} b_k\Big)\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle \displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n} (c\cdot a_k) = c\cdot\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{n} a_k\Big)\)
      🔗
      🔗
    Together with the four formulas above, these two rules let you evaluate any summation whose \(k\)th term is a polynomial in \(k\text{,}\) exactly the kind of summation a Riemann sum for a polynomial produces.
    🔗
    🔗

    2. Combining the Rules.

    Use the summation formulas and the two rules together on a sum that mixes a multiple of \(k\) with a constant.
    🔗

    (a)

    Write out \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{5}(3k-2)\) as five separate terms (substituting \(k=1,2,3,4,5\) one at a time), then add them up.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Now evaluate the same sum using the rules instead of listing terms. First use the sum rule and constant multiple rule to rewrite \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{5}(3k-2)\) as \(\displaystyle 3\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{5} k\Big) - \Big(\sum_{k=1}^{5} 2\Big)\text{,}\) then use the summation formulas to evaluate each piece.
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Do your two answers agree? Which method would you rather use if the sum had \(500\) terms instead of \(5\text{?}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Without writing out any individual terms, find \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{20}(k^2-4k)\) using only the summation formulas and rules.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3. Evaluating a Riemann Sum: \(n=20\).

    Put the summation formulas to work on an actual Riemann sum. Consider \(\displaystyle\int_1^3 x^2\,dx\text{,}\) and partition \([1,3]\) into \(n=20\) equal subintervals. Then \(\displaystyle\Delta x = \frac{1}{10}\) and the \(k\)th right endpoint is \(\displaystyle x_k = 1+\frac{k}{10}\text{,}\) so the right Riemann sum is
    \begin{equation*} \displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{20}\Big(1+\frac{k}{10}\Big)^2\cdot\frac{1}{10}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Right now this is just notation for "add up twenty numbers." Turn it into a single number without computing all twenty terms by hand.
    🔗

    (a)

    Expand the square \(\displaystyle\Big(1+\frac{k}{10}\Big)^2\text{,}\) then distribute the factor of \(\displaystyle\frac{1}{10}\text{,}\) so that the summand is written as three separate terms in powers of \(k\text{:}\) a constant term, a term with \(k\text{,}\) and a term with \(k^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Use the sum rule and constant multiple rule to split \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{20}\Big(1+\frac{k}{10}\Big)^2\cdot\frac{1}{10}\) into three separate summations, one for each term from the previous task.
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Use the four summation formulas, with \(n=20\text{,}\) to evaluate each of your three summations, then add the results to get a single number for the right Riemann sum.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    4. Generalizing to Any \(n\).

    Repeat the same process, but leave \(n\) as a symbol instead of fixing it at \(20\text{.}\) Partition \([1,3]\) into \(n\) equal subintervals. Then \(\displaystyle\Delta x = \frac{2}{n}\) and \(\displaystyle x_k = 1+\frac{2k}{n}\text{,}\) so the right Riemann sum is
    \begin{equation*} \displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Big(1+\frac{2k}{n}\Big)^2\cdot\frac{2}{n}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Be careful: \(k\) is still the index that changes from term to term, but \(n\) now plays the same role as the \(a\) or \(c\) in the summation formulas and rules: a fixed number that doesn’t change as \(k\) runs from \(1\) to \(n\text{.}\) It just happens to be a number we haven’t chosen a specific value for.
    🔗

    (a)

    Expand the square \(\displaystyle\Big(1+\frac{2k}{n}\Big)^2\text{,}\) then distribute the factor of \(\displaystyle\frac{2}{n}\text{,}\) so the summand is again written as a constant term, a term with \(k\text{,}\) and a term with \(k^2\) (each of these terms will now involve \(n\) as well).
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Use the sum rule and constant multiple rule to split the summation into three separate pieces, one for each term from the previous task, exactly as you did for \(n=20\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Use the four summation formulas to evaluate each piece in terms of \(n\text{,}\) then combine and simplify to write the right Riemann sum for \(\displaystyle\int_1^3 x^2\,dx\) as a single formula in \(n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Substitute \(n=20\) into your formula from the previous task. Does it match the number you found in the previous exercise?
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 26 Looking Ahead.

    You now have a single formula that gives the right Riemann sum for \(\displaystyle\int_1^3 x^2\,dx\) for any number of subintervals \(n\text{,}\) without adding up individual terms. In a future class, we’ll ask what happens to that formula as \(n\) grows larger and larger without bound.
