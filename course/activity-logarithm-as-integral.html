    Worksheet Properties of the Logarithm Defined by an Integral
    

    Objectives

    • Explain why \(\ln(1)=0\) and why the domain of \(\displaystyle\ln(x)=\int_1^x\frac{1}{t}\,dt\) is \((0,\infty)\text{,}\) directly from the definition.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Recognize that \(\displaystyle\int_a^{ab}\frac{1}{t}\,dt=\int_1^b\frac{1}{t}\,dt\) by comparing Riemann sums. Use this to justify \(\ln(ab)=\ln(a)+\ln(b)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Use the product rule for \(\ln\) to obtain the reciprocal rule, the quotient rule, and the power rule \(\ln(a^p)=p\ln(a)\) for integers \(p\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Use the algebraic properties of \(\ln\) to find the logarithm of a product, quotient, or power from known values of \(\ln(a)\) and \(\ln(b)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Last time we used the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus to create a new function that no power rule could produce. Today we find out what it can do. Everything we learn about this function will come from its definition as an integral, not from any formula or from anything you may remember about logarithms.
    🔗
    Team Roles: Manager, Recorder, Spokesperson, and Reflector.
    🔗

    Definition 45. The Natural Logarithm.

    For \(x\gt 0\text{,}\) the natural logarithm is defined by
    \begin{equation*} \ln(x) = \int_1^x \frac{1}{t}\,dt\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 46. Two Facts Straight from the Definition.

    First, \(\ln(1)=\displaystyle\int_1^1\frac{1}{t}\,dt=0\text{,}\) since an integral over an interval of zero width is zero. Second, the integrand \(1/t\) is not defined at \(t=0\text{,}\) and an integral starting at \(t=1\) cannot pass through that discontinuity. So the domain of \(\ln(x)\) is \((0,\infty)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 47. Algebraic Properties of \(\ln\).

    For all \(a\gt 0\) and \(b\gt 0\text{:}\)
    🔗
    • Product rule: \(\ln(ab)=\ln(a)+\ln(b)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Reciprocal rule: \(\ln\!\left(\dfrac{1}{b}\right)=-\ln(b)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Quotient rule: \(\ln\!\left(\dfrac{a}{b}\right)=\ln(a)-\ln(b)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Power rule: \(\ln(a^p)=p\,\ln(a)\) for every real number \(p\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    1. Why the Properties Hold.

    Every property in Theorem 47 follows from the definition of \(\ln\) as an integral. We will work through the first task together as a class. Your group will then use it to build the rest.
    🔗

    (a) Scaling the Interval (Whole Class).

    Let \(a\gt 0\) and \(b\gt 0\text{.}\) We want to show that
    \begin{equation*} \int_a^{ab}\frac{1}{t}\,dt = \int_1^{b}\frac{1}{t}\,dt\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Divide each interval into \(n\) subintervals of equal width. For the integral on the left, find the width \(\Delta t\) of each subinterval and the right endpoint \(t_k\) of the \(k\)th subinterval. For the integral on the right, find the width \(\Delta x\) and the right endpoint \(x_k\text{.}\) Then compare the term \(\dfrac{1}{t_k}\,\Delta t\) of the left Riemann sum with the matching term \(\dfrac{1}{x_k}\,\Delta x\) of the right Riemann sum. What happens to the factor \(a\text{?}\) What does this tell us about the two integrals?
    🔗
    🔗

    (b) The Product Rule.

    Write \(\ln(ab)\) as an integral using the definition. Split the interval of integration \([1,ab]\) into the two intervals \([1,a]\) and \([a,ab]\text{.}\) Identify each of the two resulting integrals, and explain why \(\ln(ab)=\ln(a)+\ln(b)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (c) The Reciprocal Rule.

    Let \(a\gt 0\text{.}\) First, use the product rule to rewrite \(\ln\!\left(\dfrac{1}{a}\cdot a\right)\) in terms of \(\ln\!\left(\dfrac{1}{a}\right)\) and \(\ln(a)\text{.}\) Second, simplify the product \(\dfrac{1}{a}\cdot a\) first, and evaluate \(\ln\!\left(\dfrac{1}{a}\cdot a\right)\) that way. Compare your two results. What must \(\ln\!\left(\dfrac{1}{a}\right)\) be?
    🔗
    🔗

    (d) The Quotient Rule.

    Dividing by \(b\) is the same as multiplying by the reciprocal \(\dfrac{1}{b}\text{,}\) so \(\dfrac{a}{b}=a\cdot\dfrac{1}{b}\text{.}\) Use this, together with the product rule and the reciprocal rule, to explain why \(\ln\!\left(\dfrac{a}{b}\right)=\ln(a)-\ln(b)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (e) Integer Powers.

    Use the product rule to write \(\ln(a^2)=\ln(a\cdot a)\) in terms of \(\ln(a)\text{.}\) Then write \(a^3=a\cdot a^2\) and do the same for \(\ln(a^3)\text{.}\) If \(p\) is any positive integer, explain why \(\ln(a^p)=p\,\ln(a)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (f)

    A negative integer power is a reciprocal: \(a^{-p}=\dfrac{1}{a^p}\text{.}\) If \(p\) is a positive integer, use the reciprocal rule and the previous task to find \(\ln(a^{-p})\text{.}\) Does the power rule still hold? What about \(p=0\text{?}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 48. Beyond Integer Powers.

    We can show algebraically that the power rule also works for rational powers \(p=\dfrac{m}{n}\text{.}\) When \(p\) is not rational, we actually define \(x^p\) using limits of rational numbers that approach \(p\text{.}\) The continuity of \(\ln(x)\) then guarantees that \(\ln(x^p)=p\,\ln(x)\) for every real number \(p\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    2. Using the Properties.

    Suppose that \(\ln(a)=0.75\) and \(\ln(b)=0.6\text{.}\) Use the properties in Theorem 47 to find each value.
    🔗

    (a)

    Find \(\ln(ab)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Find \(\ln\!\left(\dfrac{a}{b}\right)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Find \(\ln(a^3)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Find \(\ln\!\left(\sqrt{b}\right)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3. Combining the Properties.

    Instead of assuming specific values for \(\ln(a)\) and \(\ln(b)\text{,}\) we will expand formulas to find an answer in terms of their values using the symbols \(\ln(a)\) and \(\ln(b)\text{.}\) Each value below requires more than one property. Name the property you use at each step.
    🔗

    (a)

    Find \(\ln(a^4b^3)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Find \(\ln\!\left(\dfrac{a^2}{b^5}\right)\text{.}\)

    Find \(\ln\!\left(\sqrt{a^3b}\right)\text{.}\)
