    Worksheet The Definite Integral as a Limit of Riemann Sums
    

    Objectives

    • Use a numerical tool to confirm a closed-form Riemann sum formula against a directly computed sum, and observe how its value changes as the number of subintervals grows without bound.
      🔗
      🔗
    • State the definition of the definite integral as the limit, as \(n\to\infty\text{,}\) of a right Riemann sum.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Evaluate the limit of a closed-form Riemann sum formula by examining the behavior of each of its terms as \(n\to\infty\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Independently carry out the full process, setup, evaluation, and limit, to compute a new definite integral.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Compute the accumulation function \(\int_0^b f(x)\,dx\) for \(f(x)=x\text{,}\) \(f(x)=x^2\text{,}\) and \(f(x)=x^3\text{,}\) and recognize an emerging pattern in the results.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    In the previous activity, you partitioned \([1,3]\) into \(n\) equal subintervals and used the summation formulas to evaluate the right Riemann sum for \(\displaystyle\int_1^3 x^2\,dx\) as a single closed-form expression in \(n\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Big(1+\frac{2k}{n}\Big)^2\cdot\frac{2}{n} \;=\; \frac{26}{3}+\frac{8}{n}+\frac{4}{3n^2}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    This activity starts from that formula.
    🔗
    Team Roles: Manager, Recorder, Spokesperson, and Reflector.
    🔗

    Open the Riemann sum calculator linked below. It graphs \(f(x)=x^2\) and lets you set \(a\text{,}\) \(b\text{,}\) and \(n\) with sliders; it reports \(\Delta x\) and the value of the right Riemann sum \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^n f(a+k\Delta x)\Delta x\) directly.

    Set \(a=1\text{,}\) \(b=3\text{,}\) and \(n=20\text{.}\) Confirm that the sum Desmos reports matches what your formula from the previous activity gives when \(n=20\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Now increase \(n\text{,}\) trying several larger values. Record what happens to the value of the sum as \(n\) grows.
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Based on what you observed, what single number does the sum appear to be approaching? How does it compare to \(\frac{26}{3}\text{?}\)

    Definition 27. The Definite Integral.

    For a function \(f\) defined on an interval \([a,b]\text{,}\) partition \([a,b]\) into \(n\) equal subintervals of width \(\displaystyle\Delta x = \frac{b-a}{n}\text{,}\) and let \(x_k = a+k\Delta x\) denote the right endpoint of the \(k\)th subinterval. The definite integral of \(f\) from \(a\) to \(b\) is defined as
    \begin{equation*} \displaystyle\int_a^b f(x)\,dx \;:=\; \lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=1}^n f(x_k)\,\Delta x\text{,} \end{equation*}
    provided this limit exists.
    🔗
    Officially, the value \(f(x_k)\) must be able to be replaced by \(f(x^*)\) using any \(x^*\) between \(x_{k-1}\) and \(x_k\text{.}\) A Riemann sum is only ever an approximation, and the limit is the exact value.
    🔗
    🔗

    2. Taking the Limit.

    Apply the definition to the formula you already have for \(\displaystyle\int_1^3 x^2\,dx\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\Big(\frac{26}{3}+\frac{8}{n}+\frac{4}{3n^2}\Big) \text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗

    (a)

    Consider the two terms \(\frac{8}{n}\) and \(\frac{4}{3n^2}\) separately. What happens to each one as \(n\to\infty\text{?}\) Explain in terms of a fraction with a fixed numerator whose denominator grows without bound.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Use the previous task to evaluate \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\Big(\frac{26}{3}+ \frac{8}{n}+\frac{4}{3n^2}\Big)\text{,}\) and use the definition to state the value of \(\displaystyle\int_1^3 x^2\,dx\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    How does this value compare to the number the Desmos sum appeared to be approaching in the previous exercise?

    🔗

    3. Practice: A New Integral.

    Now carry out the entire process yourself, from setting up the Riemann sum through taking the limit, for a new integral: \(\displaystyle\int_0^3 (2x^2-5x)\,dx\text{.}\)
    🔗

    (a)

    Partition \([0,3]\) into \(n\) equal subintervals. For \(f(x)=2x^2-5x\text{,}\) write \(\Delta x\) and \(x_k\) (the right endpoint of the \(k\)th subinterval), then write the right Riemann sum for \(\displaystyle\int_0^3 (2x^2-5x)\,dx\) in summation notation.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Substitute your expression for \(x_k\) into \(f(x_k)\text{,}\) then expand and simplify so the summand is written as separate terms in powers of \(k\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Use the sum rule and constant multiple rule to split your summation into one separate summation for each term from the previous task.
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Use the summation formulas to evaluate each piece, then combine and simplify to write the right Riemann sum for \(\displaystyle\int_0^3 (2x^2-5x)\,dx\) as a single closed-form expression in \(n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (e)

    Take the limit of your formula as \(n\to\infty\text{,}\) and use the definition to state the value of \(\displaystyle\int_0^3 (2x^2-5x)\,dx\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Definition 28. Accumulation Functions.

    Every definite integral you have evaluated so far has had two fixed numbers as its limits of integration. Suppose instead the lower limit is fixed at \(a\text{,}\) but the upper limit is left as a symbol \(b\text{.}\) We can still work through the steps and get the value of the limit that depends on \(b\text{.}\) The result,
    \begin{equation*} \displaystyle A(b) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx\text{,} \end{equation*}
    is a function of \(b\text{,}\) called an accumulation function: its value at any particular \(b\) is the accumulated change, or signed area, from \(a\) up to that point.
    🔗
    🔗

    4. Three Accumulation Functions.

    Find the accumulation function based at \(a=0\) for three power functions: \(f(x)=x\text{,}\) \(f(x)=x^2\text{,}\) and \(f(x)=x^3\text{.}\) In each case \(\Delta x = b/n\) and \(x_k = kb/n\text{,}\) exactly as before but with a fixed number replaced by the symbol \(b\text{.}\) Because each of these functions is a single power of \(x\text{,}\) no expanding or splitting is needed: one summation formula does all the work.
    🔗

    (a)

    Find \(\displaystyle\int_0^b x\,dx\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Find \(\displaystyle\int_0^b x^2\,dx\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Find \(\displaystyle\int_0^b x^3\,dx\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Line up your three results. What pattern do you notice? Based on that pattern, and without computing any Riemann sum, conjecture a formula for \(\displaystyle\int_0^b x^4\,dx\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 29. Looking Ahead.

    You now have accumulation functions for three different power functions, and a conjecture for a fourth, all found the same way: by evaluating a Riemann sum and taking a limit. In an upcoming class we will see that this pattern is not a coincidence, and connect it to antiderivatives.
