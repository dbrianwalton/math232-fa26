    Worksheet What Does the Graph of the Rate Tell Us?
    

    Objectives

    • Use the graph of \(f'(x)\) to build a sign-analysis number line, and use it to determine the intervals where \(f(x)\) is increasing and decreasing.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Use the slope of the graph of \(f'(x)\) to determine the sign of \(f''(x)\text{,}\) including recognizing an interval where \(f(x)\) has no concavity at all.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Combine both sign analyses to sketch the correct shape of a graph of \(f(x)\text{,}\) and identify what information about \(f(x)\) is not determined by the graph of \(f'(x)\) alone.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    This is our first activity together using this team-based format, though you already started today’s work: at the end of last class, your group looked at the graph of \(f'(x)\) and worked through the review questions on the handout on your own. Today you’ll work in an assigned team to tackle the harder question that graph raises. As with any team task, the goal isn’t to race to an answer — it’s for everyone in the group to be able to explain why an answer is correct, not just to have written one down.
    🔗
    Each group member takes one of the following roles for the activity:
    🔗
    Role Responsibility
    Manager Keeps the group on task and on pace; makes sure everyone has spoken before the group moves on; asks the instructor for help if the group is stuck after trying on its own.
    Recorder Writes the group’s agreed-on answers, in the group’s own words; notes any question the group could not resolve.
    Spokesperson Speaks for the group during whole-class discussion and can explain why the group answered as it did, not just what was written.
    Reflector Checks in periodically on whether everyone understands, not just whether the group is moving forward; leads a short end-of-activity check-in on what worked.
    In a group of three, the Manager also takes on the Reflector’s check-in duties.
    🔗
    Last class you discussed, in general terms, what it means for a function to be increasing (or concave up), and how the sign of a derivative tells us this. Today you’ll apply those ideas to a specific function — but this time all you’re given is a picture, not a formula. Below is the same graph of \(f'(x)\) from last class’s handout, the derivative of an unknown function \(f(x)\text{.}\)
    🔗

    1. Where Is f Increasing or Decreasing?

    Start with what you already know: the sign of \(f'(x)\) tells you whether \(f(x)\) is increasing or decreasing. Here you have to read that sign straight from the picture.
    🔗

    (a)

    Find every value of \(x\) where the graph of \(f'(x)\) crosses the \(x\)-axis (roots). List them in increasing order.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Draw a number line for \(x\) running from \(-12\) to \(10\text{,}\) marked with the values you just found. On each interval between them, record whether \(f'(x)\) is positive or negative. How do you read this off the graph without computing anything.
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Translate your sign chart into a statement about \(f(x)\text{:}\) on which intervals is \(f(x)\) increasing? On which is it decreasing? At which \(x\)-values does \(f(x)\) have a local maximum or local minimum, and how do you know from the chart alone?
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Suppose two different functions, \(f(x)\) and \(g(x)\text{,}\) happened to have this exact same derivative graph. Would your sign chart above be the same for both of them? Explain.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2. Where Is f Concave Up, Concave Down, or Neither?

    You also know that the sign of \(f''(x)\) tells you the concavity of \(f(x)\text{.}\) But you were not given a graph of \(f''(x)\) — only the graph of \(f'(x)\text{.}\) Discuss with your group: what feature of the graph you already have could tell you the sign of \(f''(x)\text{,}\) without ever seeing a graph of \(f''(x)\) itself?
    🔗

    (a)

    The graph of \(f'(x)\) is made of several straight-line pieces. For each piece, decide whether the graph is increasing (rising left-to-right), decreasing (falling left-to-right), or constant (flat). Record the intervals where each occurs.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Use your answer above to build a second number line for \(x\) from \(-12\) to \(10\text{,}\) this time recording the sign of \(f''(x)\) on each interval. Note any interval where \(f''(x)\) is zero the whole way across, rather than just at a single point.
    🔗
    What is happening at the end points of the intervals?
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Focus on the interval where \(f''(x) = 0\) throughout. What does this tell you about the shape of \(f(x)\) on that interval? Is it concave up, concave down, or something else? Describe, in words, what that piece of the graph of \(f(x)\) must look like.
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Write a general rule, in your own words, that connects a feature of the graph of \(f'(x)\) to the sign of \(f''(x)\text{,}\) a rule you could apply to any graph of a derivative, not just this one.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3. Sketching the Shape of f.

    Now put both sign charts together.
    🔗

    (a)

    Combine your two number lines into a single chart marking every \(x\)-value where something changes: where \(f\) switches between increasing and decreasing, and where \(f\) switches concavity (or loses concavity entirely).
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Using the combined chart, sketch a graph of \(y = f(x)\) that shows the correct shape on \(-12 < x < 10\text{:}\) every rise, fall, and change in bend should match your chart. Don’t worry about labeling any values on the vertical axis.
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Look at the sketch your group drew. Can you determine the actual \(y\)-coordinate of any point on it — including the local maximum or minimum values? Why or why not?
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    On the same axes (or a second copy of the same axes), sketch a different function that has this exact same graph of \(f'(x)\text{,}\) but that is not the same function \(f(x)\) you sketched above. Compare your two sketches: what has to be identical between them? What is free to be different?
    🔗
