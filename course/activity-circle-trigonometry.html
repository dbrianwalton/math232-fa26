    Worksheet Circle Trigonometry
    

    Objectives

    • Explain why the opposite vertex of a unit right triangle with a vertex at the origin has coordinates \((\cos(\theta),\sin(\theta))\text{,}\) and connect the Pythagorean theorem to the unit circle equation \(x^2+y^2=1\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Locate the terminal ray of any angle by rotating from \((1,0)\text{,}\) counterclockwise for positive angles and clockwise for negative angles, and use its point on the unit circle to define \(\sin(\theta)\) and \(\cos(\theta)\) for every angle.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Define \(\tan(\theta)\text{,}\) \(\sec(\theta)\text{,}\) \(\cot(\theta)\text{,}\) and \(\csc(\theta)\) as ratios of the coordinates, and decide where each is undefined.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Find all six trigonometric values of an angle by combining a reference triangle with the signs of \(x\) and \(y\) in the quadrant of the terminal ray.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Use \(x^2+y^2=1\) and the quadrant to find all six trigonometric values when only one coordinate is known.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Explain why sine is an odd function and cosine is an even function, using the symmetry of the unit circle.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Last time we rescaled right triangles so that the hypotenuse had length \(1\text{,}\) and found that the other two sides are \(\cos(\theta)\) and \(\sin(\theta)\text{.}\) That works only for acute angles, because a right triangle cannot have an angle of \(150^\circ\) or \(-40^\circ\text{.}\) Today we place the unit triangle inside a circle. The circle will let us turn what we observed for angles between \(0^\circ\) and \(90^\circ\) into a definition that works for every angle.
    🔗

    1. Unit Triangles Around the Origin.

    The figure shows several right triangles whose hypotenuse has length \(1\text{.}\) Each has one vertex at the origin \((0,0)\) and one leg along the \(x\)-axis. The vertex opposite the origin is a point \(P=(x,y)\) on the circle of radius \(r=1\) centered at the origin, called the unit circle. The highlighted triangle has the acute angle \(\theta\) at the origin.
    🔗

    (a)

    Using the unit triangle with hypotenuse \(1\) from the previous activity, what are the lengths of the horizontal and vertical legs of the highlighted triangle? Explain why the point \(P\) has coordinates \((\cos(\theta),\sin(\theta))\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Apply the Pythagorean theorem to the highlighted triangle to get an equation relating the coordinates \(x\) and \(y\) of \(P\text{.}\) What familiar equation is this? Which identity from the previous activity does it give when written using \(\cos(\theta)\) and \(\sin(\theta)\text{?}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    What happens to the triangle as \(\theta\) gets smaller and smaller? Which point on the circle should we assign to the angle \(\theta=0^\circ\text{?}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Moving along the circle from \((1,0)\) to \(P\) covers a distance called the arc length. This distance is a fraction of the full circumference \(2\pi r=2\pi\text{,}\) and the fraction is the same as the fraction of a full revolution (\(360^\circ\)) that the angle covers. Find the arc length for \(\theta=90^\circ\) and for \(\theta=45^\circ\text{.}\) Then write a formula for the arc length when the angle is \(\theta\) degrees.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 85 Trigonometric Functions of Any Angle.

    To find the point for an angle \(\theta\text{,}\) start at \((1,0)\) on the unit circle and move along the circle to cover the angle. If \(\theta\gt 0\text{,}\) rotate in the counterclockwise direction. If \(\theta\lt 0\text{,}\) rotate in the clockwise direction through \(|\theta|\text{.}\) The ray from \((0,0)\) through the point reached is the terminal ray of the angle. If the point is \((x,y)\text{,}\) then we define
    \begin{align*} \cos(\theta) \amp = x \amp \sin(\theta) \amp = y \end{align*}
    and define the other four trigonometric functions as ratios of these coordinates:
    \begin{align*} \tan(\theta) \amp = \frac{y}{x} = \frac{\sin(\theta)}{\cos(\theta)} \amp \sec(\theta) \amp = \frac{1}{x} = \frac{1}{\cos(\theta)} \\ \cot(\theta) \amp = \frac{x}{y} = \frac{\cos(\theta)}{\sin(\theta)} \amp \csc(\theta) \amp = \frac{1}{y} = \frac{1}{\sin(\theta)} \end{align*}
    whenever the denominator is not zero.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 86 Reversing Our Observation.

    For an acute angle, this definition agrees with the right triangle ratios, because we just saw that the point is \((\cos(\theta),\sin(\theta))\) when \(\theta\) is acute. The difference is the direction of the logic. Before, the triangle defined the coordinates. Now the coordinates define sine and cosine, and they do so for angles of any size or sign. Every point on the unit circle satisfies \(x^2+y^2=1\text{,}\) so
    \begin{equation*} \cos^2(\theta)+\sin^2(\theta)=1 \end{equation*}
    holds for every angle \(\theta\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    2. Signs in the Four Quadrants.

    The coordinate axes divide the plane into four quadrants, numbered I through IV counterclockwise, with Quadrant I in the upper right.

    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    🔗

    Two Special Triangles.

    Two right triangles with hypotenuse \(1\) have side lengths that we can find exactly.
    🔗
    The \(45^\circ\)-\(45^\circ\)-\(90^\circ\) triangle. The two acute angles are equal, so the triangle is isosceles and both legs have the same length \(x\text{.}\) The Pythagorean theorem gives \(x^2+x^2=1\text{,}\) so \(x^2=\dfrac{1}{2}\) and \(x=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}=\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The \(30^\circ\)-\(60^\circ\)-\(90^\circ\) triangle. An equilateral triangle with side length \(1\) can have one angle bisected to create an angle of \(30^\circ\text{.}\) The bisector cuts the opposite side in half, so the short leg of each right triangle is \(\dfrac{1}{2}\text{.}\) The Pythagorean theorem then gives the long leg \(\sqrt{1-\dfrac{1}{4}}=\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example: The Angle 150 Degrees.

    Find \(\sin(150^\circ)\) and \(\cos(150^\circ)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The angle \(150^\circ\) is not acute, so it is not an angle of a right triangle. Instead, we rotate \(150^\circ\) counterclockwise from \((1,0)\) and mark the point \((x,y)\) on the unit circle. This point is in Quadrant II. If we draw a vertical line from the point to the \(x\)-axis, we get a reference triangle. Its angle at the origin is \(180^\circ-150^\circ=30^\circ\text{,}\) so its horizontal leg has length \(\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\) and its vertical leg has length \(\dfrac{1}{2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    In Quadrant II we have \(x\lt 0\) and \(y\gt 0\text{,}\) so \((x,y)=\left(-\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2},\dfrac{1}{2}\right)\text{.}\) Therefore \(\cos(150^\circ)=-\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\) and \(\sin(150^\circ)=\dfrac{1}{2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 87 What “Using the Unit Circle” Means.

    When we say we are using the unit circle, we are not consulting a memorized list of points built from \(30^\circ\text{,}\) \(45^\circ\text{,}\) and \(60^\circ\) reference triangles. We are using three ideas together: the equation \(x^2+y^2=1\) for points on the circle, the definitions \(\cos(\theta)=x\) and \(\sin(\theta)=y\text{,}\) and the signs of \(x\) and \(y\) determined by the quadrant of the terminal ray. The special triangles are the cases where the side lengths happen to be especially simple.

    Theorem 88 Sine Is Odd and Cosine Is Even.

    For every angle \(\theta\text{,}\)
    \begin{align*} \sin(-\theta) \amp = -\sin(\theta) \\ \cos(-\theta) \amp = \cos(\theta)\text{.} \end{align*}
    That is, \(\sin\) is an odd function and \(\cos\) is an even function.
    🔗
