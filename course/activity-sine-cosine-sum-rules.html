    Worksheet Sum Rules for Sine and Cosine
    

    Objectives

    • Interpret adding angles as following one rotation by another, and use the sum identities for sine and cosine to find the coordinates of a rotated point.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Apply the sum identities by replacing \(\sin(\theta)\text{,}\) \(\cos(\theta)\text{,}\) \(\sin(\varphi)\text{,}\) and \(\cos(\varphi)\) with known coordinate values, to find the sine and cosine of angles such as \(75^\circ\text{,}\) \(15^\circ\text{,}\) and \(\theta+60^\circ\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Use the sum identities in the limit definition of the derivative to write \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\sin(x)\) and \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\cos(x)\) in terms of \(\sin(x)\text{,}\) \(\cos(x)\text{,}\) and two limits in \(h\text{,}\) and identify those two limits.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Last time we defined sine and cosine as the coordinates of a point on the unit circle. To differentiate these functions we will need to know how \(\sin(x+h)\) and \(\cos(x+h)\) relate to the values at \(x\) and at \(h\text{.}\) We begin by seeing that adding angles means following one rotation by another. This gives the sum identities for sine and cosine, which we then put to work in the limit definition of the derivative.
    🔗

    1. Rotating Other Starting Points.

    Rotating the starting point \((1,0)\) by an angle \(\theta\) moves it to the point \((\cos(\theta),\sin(\theta))\) on the unit circle. Where do other starting points go under the same rotation?
    🔗

    (a)

    Where does the point \((2,0)\) move when it is rotated by the angle \(\theta\text{?}\) Explain your answer using the fact that \((2,0)\) is twice as far from the origin as \((1,0)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Sketch the unit circle and the point \((0,1)\text{.}\) Rotate this point by an acute angle \(\theta\) and draw a reference triangle for the new point. Use the triangle to find the coordinates of the new point in terms of \(\cos(\theta)\) and \(\sin(\theta)\text{.}\) Pay attention to where the angle \(\theta\) sits in your triangle and to which side of the \(y\)-axis the new point lies on.
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Where does the point \((0,3)\) move when it is rotated by the angle \(\theta\text{?}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Rotating by Components.

    We can reach a point \((a,b)\) from the origin in two steps: a horizontal displacement \(a\text{,}\) and then a vertical displacement \(b\text{.}\) As vectors, this says
    \begin{equation*} (a,b) = (a,0) + (0,b)\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Rotation respects this splitting. To rotate the point \((a,b)\text{,}\) we can rotate the two displacements \((a,0)\) and \((0,b)\) separately, and then add the results.
    🔗
    🔗

    Adding Angles Means Adding Rotations.

    Rotate the point \((1,0)\) by an angle \(\theta\text{,}\) and then continue by rotating through a further angle \(\varphi\text{.}\) The total rotation is by the angle \(\theta+\varphi\text{,}\) so the point we reach is \((\cos(\theta+\varphi),\sin(\theta+\varphi))\text{.}\) We can also find this point in pieces, following the four pictures below.
    🔗
    The basic rotations by \(\varphi\) are in Figure 89. After the rotation by \(\theta\) we are at \((\cos(\theta),\sin(\theta)) = (\cos(\theta),0)+(0,\sin(\theta))\text{.}\) Rotating each piece by \(\varphi\) gives the two points in Figure 90 and Figure 91:
    \begin{align*} (\cos(\theta),0) \amp \longrightarrow \bigl(\cos(\theta)\cos(\varphi),\ \cos(\theta)\sin(\varphi)\bigr) \\ (0,\sin(\theta)) \amp \longrightarrow \bigl(-\sin(\theta)\sin(\varphi),\ \sin(\theta)\cos(\varphi)\bigr) \end{align*}
    Adding the two rotated pieces, as in Figure 92, reaches the point
    \begin{equation*} \bigl(\cos(\theta)\cos(\varphi)-\sin(\theta)\sin(\varphi),\ \sin(\theta)\cos(\varphi)+\cos(\theta)\sin(\varphi)\bigr)\text{.} \end{equation*}
    This is the same point as \((\cos(\theta+\varphi),\sin(\theta+\varphi))\text{,}\) so the coordinates must match.
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 93 Sum Identities for Sine and Cosine.

    For all angles \(\theta\) and \(\varphi\text{,}\)
    \begin{align*} \sin(\theta+\varphi) \amp = \sin(\theta)\cos(\varphi)+\cos(\theta)\sin(\varphi) \\ \cos(\theta+\varphi) \amp = \cos(\theta)\cos(\varphi)-\sin(\theta)\sin(\varphi)\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 94 Replace the Four Quantities.

    In Theorem 93, the four quantities \(\sin(\theta)\text{,}\) \(\cos(\theta)\text{,}\) \(\sin(\varphi)\text{,}\) and \(\cos(\varphi)\) are numbers: they are the coordinates of two points on the unit circle. We use the identities by replacing each of these four quantities with its known coordinate value. We never put a coordinate value inside a sine or cosine. For example, to find \(\sin(45^\circ+30^\circ)\) we do not evaluate \(\sin\bigl(\tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\bigr)\text{.}\) We replace \(\sin(45^\circ)\) by \(\tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\text{,}\) replace \(\cos(30^\circ)\) by \(\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\text{,}\) and so on, and then combine the numbers.
    🔗
    🔗

    2. Adding Rotations in Practice.

    Use the values of sine and cosine at \(30^\circ\text{,}\) \(45^\circ\text{,}\) and \(60^\circ\) from Two Special Triangles.
    🔗

    (a)

    Take \(\theta=60^\circ\) and \(\varphi=30^\circ\text{.}\) Write down the four numbers that replace \(\sin(\theta)\text{,}\) \(\cos(\theta)\text{,}\) \(\sin(\varphi)\text{,}\) and \(\cos(\varphi)\text{,}\) and then use Theorem 93 to compute \(\sin(90^\circ)\) and \(\cos(90^\circ)\text{.}\) Do they agree with the point you found for \(90^\circ\) earlier?
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Write \(75^\circ\) as the sum of two angles from our special triangles. Use Theorem 93 to find \(\sin(75^\circ)\) and \(\cos(75^\circ)\) exactly.
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    What about \(15^\circ\text{?}\) It is not the sum of two of our special angles, but it is a sum if one of the two angles is negative. Use Theorem 88 to find the sine and cosine of the negative angle, and then find \(\sin(15^\circ)\) and \(\cos(15^\circ)\) exactly.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3. Rotating a Point Further by 60 Degrees.

    An angle \(\theta\) has its terminal ray in Quadrant II, and \(\sin(\theta)=\dfrac{2}{5}\text{.}\) We want the coordinates of the point on the unit circle for the angle \(\theta+60^\circ\text{.}\) We do not know the angle \(\theta\) itself, and we do not need it.
    🔗

    (a)

    Use the unit circle equation and the quadrant to find \(\cos(\theta)\) exactly.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Find \(\sin(60^\circ)\) and \(\cos(60^\circ)\) from the reference triangle.
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Use Theorem 93 to find \(\cos(\theta+60^\circ)\) and \(\sin(\theta+60^\circ)\text{,}\) and state the coordinates of the point for \(\theta+60^\circ\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    4. The Derivative of Sine.

    By the definition of the derivative, the derivative of \(f(x)=\sin(x)\) is
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d}{dx}\sin(x) = \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\sin(x+h)-\sin(x)}{h}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    The obstacle is \(\sin(x+h)\text{,}\) the sine of a sum. Theorem 93 gives us a way to handle it.
    🔗

    (a)

    Apply Theorem 93 to \(\sin(x+h)\text{,}\) with \(\theta=x\) and \(\varphi=h\text{.}\) Write the difference quotient \(\dfrac{\sin(x+h)-\sin(x)}{h}\) using your result.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    The expression \(\sin(x)\) appears in two of the terms. Regroup so that the difference quotient takes the form
    \begin{equation*} \sin(x)\cdot\bigl[\ \cdots\ \bigr] + \cos(x)\cdot\bigl[\ \cdots\ \bigr] \end{equation*}
    where each bracket is a single fraction with denominator \(h\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    As \(h\to 0\text{,}\) the value of \(x\) stays fixed. Which parts of your expression depend on \(h\text{,}\) and which do not? Write \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\sin(x)\) as \(\sin(x)\) times one limit plus \(\cos(x)\) times another limit.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    5. The Derivative of Cosine.

    The derivative of \(g(x)=\cos(x)\) is
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d}{dx}\cos(x) = \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\cos(x+h)-\cos(x)}{h}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    The plan is the same as for sine, now using the identity for \(\cos(x+h)\text{.}\)
    🔗

    (a)

    Apply Theorem 93 to \(\cos(x+h)\text{,}\) and regroup the difference quotient \(\dfrac{\cos(x+h)-\cos(x)}{h}\) so that \(\cos(x)\) multiplies one fraction with denominator \(h\) and \(\sin(x)\) multiplies another.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Write \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\cos(x)\) as \(\cos(x)\) times one limit plus (or minus) \(\sin(x)\) times another limit.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    6. Which Limits Do We Need?

    (a)

    Compare your results for sine and for cosine. Two limits in \(h\) appear in both. Write them down, and name them \(A=\displaystyle\lim_{h\to 0}\dfrac{\cos(h)-1}{h}\) and \(B=\displaystyle\lim_{h\to 0}\dfrac{\sin(h)}{h}\) if you have not already. Then write both \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\sin(x)\) and \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\cos(x)\) in terms of \(A\text{,}\) \(B\text{,}\) \(\sin(x)\text{,}\) and \(\cos(x)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    What happens to the numerator and denominator of each limit as \(h\to 0\text{?}\) Can we find \(A\) or \(B\) by substituting \(h=0\text{?}\)
