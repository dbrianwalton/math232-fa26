    On the previous worksheet, you found the accumulation function \(\displaystyle A(b) = \int_0^b f(x)\,dx\) for \(f(x)=1\text{,}\) \(x\text{,}\) \(x^2\text{,}\) and \(x^3\text{,}\) each by setting up a right Riemann sum and taking its limit as \(n\to\infty\text{.}\) Now you’ll put those four results to work: combining them to get the accumulation function for an entire polynomial, then using that to evaluate definite integrals without ever taking another limit.

    Remark 31. Reminder: Sum Rule and Constant Multiple Rule.

    Recall that the definite integral satisfies a sum rule and a constant multiple rule:
    \begin{align*} \int_a^b [f(x)+g(x)]\,dx \amp = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx + \int_a^b g(x)\,dx,\\ \int_a^b k\,f(x)\,dx \amp = k\int_a^b f(x)\,dx. \end{align*}
    These rules apply just as well when the upper limit is the symbol \(b\text{,}\) which means they apply directly to accumulation functions. Combined with the four basic formulas above, they let you build the accumulation function for any polynomial of degree at most \(3\text{,}\) one term at a time. For example, for \(p(x) = 3x^2-4x+1\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} \int_0^b p(x)\,dx \amp = 3\int_0^b x^2\,dx - 4\int_0^b x\,dx + \int_0^b 1\,dx \\ \amp = 3\cdot\frac{b^3}{3} - 4\cdot\frac{b^2}{2} + b \\ \amp = b^3-2b^2+b \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 32. Reminder: The Splitting Property.

    Recall the splitting property from the same earlier activity:
    \begin{equation*} \displaystyle\int_0^a f(x)\,dx + \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = \int_0^b f(x)\,dx\text{,} \end{equation*}
    for any numbers \(a\) and \(b\text{.}\) Since \(A(a) = \int_0^a f(x)\,dx\) and \(A(b) = \int_0^b f(x)\,dx\text{,}\) solving for the middle term gives a way to evaluate any definite integral once you know its accumulation function:
    \begin{equation*} \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = A(b) - A(a)\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
